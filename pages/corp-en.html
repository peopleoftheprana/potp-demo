On your own floor, it probably looks like this: your steadiest people are the ones closest to the edge, breaks get skipped because nobody can be spared, night shift sleeps badly and day shift snaps, and managers absorb everyone’s stress with nowhere to put their own.

Most staff wellness is built on information: a lunch-and-learn, a webinar about stress, an app with a great launch month. People leave knowing more about stress and feeling no different, because understanding stress and being able to shift it are two different things. The second is a skill, and it’s learned the way your staff learned CPR: by practicing it until the body knows what to do.

Information about stress doesn’t change a nervous system. Practice does.

We train their breath.

  • The breath

    Slow and steady, working toward 4 to 10 breaths a minute. Studies link this pace with feeling calmer, more alert and more energized, with less anxiety.

  • The beat

    A steady rhythm by composer Ma’amo Koba. Rhythm is soothing, and the body picks up the pace by feel instead of by effort. Staff follow along, and before they know it they’re breathing slower.

  • The count

    The track counts out loud, so a wandering, anxious or tired mind has one simple job: follow along.

Fast, shallow breathing through the upper chest is part of the stress response, and most people working in care breathe that way all shift without noticing. Slowing the breath pulls the other way. A 2018 review linked slow breathing, under 10 breaths a minute, with more relaxation and alertness and fewer symptoms of anxiety (Frontiers in Human Neuroscience). A 2023 Stanford trial found that five minutes a day of structured breathing for a month lifted mood and lowered breathing rate more than mindfulness meditation did (Cell Reports Medicine).

Practice it on the easy shifts, so it’s there on the hard ones.

Is this therapy?

No. BEAT + BREATH™ is education and practice. It sits alongside your EAP and clinical supports and doesn’t replace them. Esther brings a psychotherapist’s eye to how it’s taught, and nobody is assessed or treated.

Why diaphragmatic breathing?

The diaphragm is the main muscle of breathing: a dome under the lungs that flattens as you breathe in, so the belly and lower ribs move. Under stress, most people shift up into the chest and lean on the neck and shoulder muscles, which keeps the breath fast and shallow. Every BEAT + BREATH™ practice starts low and slow, from the diaphragm, so staff retrain the everyday breath and not just the breath they take in a session.

In a small 2017 trial of 40 adults, people who practiced diaphragmatic breathing over eight weeks showed better sustained attention, lower cortisol (a stress hormone) and less negative mood afterwards, while the comparison group’s cortisol didn’t change (Frontiers in Psychology).

What does the research say about slow breathing?

The research is growing, and it points in the same direction. A 2018 systematic review found that breathing under 10 breaths a minute was linked with more comfort, relaxation, vigour and alertness, and fewer symptoms of anxiety, anger and confusion (Frontiers in Human Neuroscience). A 2022 meta-analysis found that slow breathing raises heart rate variability, a marker of how easily the body shifts out of a stress response, during practice, right after a session and after weeks of training (Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews).

A 2023 meta-analysis of randomized trials found breathwork lowered self-reported stress, anxiety and depression, with small to medium effects (Scientific Reports). And a 2023 Stanford trial found five minutes a day of structured breathing for a month lifted mood and lowered breathing rate more than mindfulness meditation did (Cell Reports Medicine).

Won’t slowing down make people sleepy?

No. Slow breathing isn’t sleepy breathing. In the 2018 review above, slow breathing was linked with feeling more alert and more vigorous at the same time as more relaxed: calm and switched on, which is the state people do their best work in. Brain imaging research also links attention to the breath with stronger communication between the prefrontal cortex, which plans and decides, and the amygdala, the brain’s alarm centre (NeuroImage, 2016). Under pressure, stress pulls the thinking brain offline. Practiced breathing is how staff bring it back.

Is it safe for staff with anxiety or a trauma history?

The method was designed by a psychotherapist with exactly those staff in mind. Everyone starts with Basic and Calm Breath, which have no pauses, and the track does the counting so nobody has to push. We practice sitting or lying down, never while driving, and anyone who feels lightheaded stops and breathes normally.

Our staff don’t have time for one more thing.

That’s why the shortest track is three minutes: long enough to work, short enough for the walk between rooms, the car before a shift or the last minutes of a break. Live sessions are recorded, so nights and days off aren’t left out.
